# World Lore — The Great Entanglement

## The Day Nothing Exploded

On an ordinary afternoon in 2086, the **Meridian Exchange** unveiled the first public Time Engine. Engineers expected to send a messenger a minute into the past. Instead, the engine recognized that time and space were descriptions of the same thing, and began treating **centuries as geographic neighbors**.

No cities were bombed. No civilizations were erased. The peasants were still farming when an electric aircraft appeared above their mill. The aircraft was still obeying traffic laws. The pilot thought the runway had been replaced by a castle. Both were right.

The Exchange called it **The Great Entanglement**. The first medieval crown issued a new customs tax on future batteries within an hour. A prehistoric hunting circle learned to use empty radio signals as a predator-call. A future megacorp tried to patent agricultural seasons. For the first time, nobody could decide whether their rivals were ancestors, invaders, or descendants.

A slogan spread between eras: **“Nobody inherits history. Everyone bargains for it.”**

## The Crownless March — the starting world

The player wakes in the year **1278**, in a borderland of stone keeps, taverns, pilgrim roads, fertile woods, and absurd imports: a knight with night-vision goggles; a blacksmith selling an anti-gravity horseshoe; an oracle negotiating with a local beekeeper. The March is politically leaderless: its throne has been claimed by people who have not been born yet. The nearest war is over eleven bells that ring simultaneously in five different centuries.

The era is old-timey and futurist without being a retro-futurism paint job. Contradiction is civic infrastructure: inns accept energy cells as payment, a monastery secures its library with rotating quantum keys, a windmill powers a field shield by turning an electromagnetic axle.

## The factions and their motives

| Power | What they want | What makes them dangerous |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ashen Thrones** (1278) | Turn monarchy into the law of every age | Fealty oaths, fortifications, mounted flanks, analog traps |
| **Rootbound Circles** (12,400 BCE) | Keep the wilderness outside temporal ownership | Smell, memory, stealth, ambush, anti-prediction deception |
| **Brass Syndicate** (1889) | Industrialize chronology and own the gates | Self-contained clockwork, rail supply, siege engines |
| **Signal Houses** (2086) | Own communication between eras | Recon networks, infiltration, electric weapons |
| **Oracle Consortium** (8420) | Precompute a single stable future | Synthetic armies, matter shaping, predictive command |
| **Iron Wardens** (cross-era) | Protect ordinary people regardless of century | Fortified humanitarian outposts, disciplined forces |
| **Freefolk Union** (cross-era) | Keep gateways and trade free | Smuggling, mixed bands, unusual partnerships |
| **Pale Archive** (cross-era) | Preserve every history without choosing a ruler | Relics, copied memories, paradox research |

No entire era is good or evil. The player can ally with individuals, betray armies, trade between enemies, or unite otherwise impossible coalitions. A king can command a hover tank; a commoner can sell it to a machine philosopher.

## Story secrets and campaign arcs (design)

- **The Eleven Bells:** eleven bells distributed across five times. Each bell marks a different ending to the same siege. Who is ringing them before the siege begins?
- **The Unwritten Child:** a future oracle loses to an untrained child with a stick. Its prediction engine records the incident as the first true new event since the invention of the Time Engine.
- **The Clockmaker's Paradox:** in 1889 a mechanical engineer builds a device that could only have been invented if the future existed first. The missing blueprint appears in a monastery built 600 years earlier.
- **The Great Claim:** the player eventually chooses whether to seal borders, open all eras freely or establish a voluntary cross-era charter. Intended to affect settlements, trade, and which armies can cross gates.

### The rule of narrative consistency

Travel does not erase the present. It brings entire sovereign territories into proximity. An ordinary historical character remains a person, not a temporary power-up. The past can win through stubborn independence, tactical deception, craft, numbers and physical logistics. The future wins through processing, range, control and synthesis. **Chronofold breaks both advantages locally.**
